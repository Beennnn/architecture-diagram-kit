Briefing note · state of the art

What the research says about diagrams

There is a literature on the design of visual notations, and it is largely ignored by the people who make diagrams. Its most consistent result: what decides whether a diagram reads is almost never what you spend time on. Here are the authoritative sources, what they establish, and what they imply for our set of lockups.

01The frame of reference

Moody, The “Physics” of Notations, IEEE TSE, 2009

This is the founding paper, and it remains without a real competitor. Moody starts from a harsh observation: in software engineering, the design effort for a notation goes almost entirely into semantics — what the symbols mean — and the graphical form is handled at the end, by eye. The discipline has mature methods for evaluating a semantics, and none for evaluating a visual syntax.

He proposes nine principles, derived from perception theory and cognitive psychology. Four concern us directly.

Dual Coding — text and graphics together are more effective than either alone. Text is not an admission that the graphic failed: the two channels reinforce each other.

Graphic Economy — the number of graphically distinct symbols must stay within the limits of human discrimination. Beyond that, the notation stops being readable at a glance.

Perceptual Discriminability — two symbols must be separated by a sufficient visual distance, measurable on the visual variables in use.

Semantic Transparency — the form of a symbol should suggest its meaning to someone who has never seen it.

Daniel L. Moody, “The Physics of Notations: Toward a Scientific Basis for Constructing Visual Notations in Software Engineering”, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering 35(6), 2009, pp. 756–779.
Two decisions already taken find their theoretical justification here, and it is not a retrospective coincidence: Dual Coding validates the lockup — sign and name, not one or the other. Graphic Economy validates six layer colours rather than thirty-two. The literature says the same thing we had deduced from practice.
Perceptual Discriminability is the principle our palette breaks: under deuteranopia, web and api have a visual distance of zero. ADR 0001 accepts that on the grounds that the name carries the information — which is precisely the Dual Coding argument. But it has to be said plainly: we compensate for a weakness with another principle, we do not correct it.

02What the empirical work measured

Purchase et al. — layout weighs more than notation

Where Moody theorises, Purchase measures. Her series of experiments on UML class diagrams varies five layout properties and measures actual comprehension performance: accuracy of answers, time, visual effort.

The result is clear and it has not moved in twenty-five years. Minimising edge crossings is by far the most important aesthetic — “by far the most important”, ahead of symmetry, angular resolution and orthogonality. More recent work on weighted-vertex graphs finds the same ranking.

On an architecture diagram, redrawing the layout to remove three crossings does more for readability than all the iconography work put together. Our lockups will never redeem a badly laid-out graph.

Secondary notation

Petre, in a paper that has become a classic, shows something else: what distinguishes the expert from the novice in reading a diagram is not knowledge of the notation but mastery of the secondary notation — alignment, white space, spatial grouping, everything defined by no formal semantics and that the author often produces without thinking.

Novices fail not because they do not know what the symbols mean, but because they lack the reading and navigation strategies the expert applies to the layout. Performance depends heavily on the layout.

Marian Petre, “Why Looking Isn't Always Seeing: Readership Skills and Graphical Programming”, Communications of the ACM 38(6), 1995.

A practical and counter-intuitive consequence: aligning the boxes that belong together, and separating with white space those that have nothing to do with each other, is a semantic act. The reader will decode it, whether you meant it or not. An accidental alignment lies.

03Why the name must touch the sign

Split-attention effect, spatial contiguity

This is the most solidly established result of the note, because it rests on a meta-analysis and not on an isolated study.

The spatial contiguity principle states that people learn better when the words and the images that refer to one another are spatially close. Its reverse, the split-attention effect, measures the degradation caused by having to mentally integrate separated sources of information.

A random-effects meta-analysis over 58 independent comparisons (n = 2,426) establishes an overall effect size of g = 0.63 (p < 0.001) — a medium to strong effect, in educational science.

Schroeder & Cenkci, “Spatial Contiguity and Spatial Split-Attention Effects in Multimedia Learning Environments: a Meta-Analysis”, Educational Psychology Review, 2018. See also Ayres & Sweller, The Split-Attention Principle, Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning.

An important nuance: the effect is conditioned by the complexity of the material. On simple content, proximity changes little. On content with strong interactivity between elements — which is exactly what an architecture diagram is — the effect is strong.

A legend in the margin associating a colour with a layer imposes a split of attention: the reader has to go back and forth and memorise. The name written inside the pill removes it. That is the empirical justification for choosing “label” over “symbol alone”, and it is stronger than the aesthetic argument.

04The practical consensus

C4, arc42, Views & Beyond — what everyone agrees on

Simon Brown's C4 model is today the most widely used framework for software architecture diagrams. Its main contribution is not graphical: it is abstraction-first, the idea that you choose the level of abstraction first — context, container, component, code — and that a diagram never mixes two.

A notable fact, often misunderstood: C4 is deliberately notation-independent. It prescribes neither shape, nor colour, nor arrow style. It states one requirement on form, and it is absolute:

Every diagram must carry a key / legend explaining the notation used — shapes, colours, line styles, arrow types, terminations.

Simon Brown, the C4 model, c4model.com/diagrams/notation. The model took shape between 2006 and 2011; book: The C4 Model: Visualizing Software Architecture.

The rest of the consensus, found identically in arc42 and in the SEI's Documenting Software Architectures:

  • A title on every diagram, saying the type and the scope.
  • Every element carries a name, a type, a technology and a sentence of description.
  • Every arrow is directed and named — “reads the orders from”, not “uses”.
  • No unexplained acronym.
  • The diagram must be understood on its own, without its author beside it.
Our set serves the “technology” point: that is exactly what a lockup encodes. But it exempts none of the other rules — least of all the legend, since our six layer colours are a convention the reader cannot guess.

05The consequence for draw.io

Who should draw the name: the SVG, or draw.io?

The question looks technical. It is in fact settled by section 03, and the answer is the opposite of the intuition.

We produce lockups with vectorised text — self-contained, identical everywhere, no font to install. That is the right answer for a sheet, a README, a slide, a Figma file: contexts with no composition layer.

In draw.io it is the wrong answer. Because draw.io can compose, and vectorised text loses four properties there:

Hence the construction: a draw.io .xml library — an <mxlibrary> node containing a JSON array — where every entry carries the symbol alone as a data:image/svg+xml URI, plus a title that becomes the label, and a style fixing the layer colour in fontColor. Separate libraries — protocols, products, roles and the grammar — so the picker stays navigable.

The two sets do not compete, they serve two contexts. draw.io composes → give it the sign and it writes the name. A slide does not compose → give it the baked lockup. The repository produces both.

06The review protocol

In the order of measured impact, not in the order of comfort

  1. Count the arrow crossings, and remove them.The strongest lever, and the one always skipped. Moving a box costs ten seconds.
  2. Check that every alignment is intentional.Two aligned boxes say “we belong together”. If that is false, break the alignment.
  3. One level of abstraction per diagram.A container and a class on the same picture, and the reader no longer knows where they are.
  4. Name every arrow, and direct it.“reads the orders from” carries information; “uses” carries none.
  5. A legend, always.Our six layer colours are an arbitrary convention. Without a legend they mean nothing.
  6. Expand every acronym.Including the ones “everybody knows” on your team.
  7. Have someone from outside read it, with no commentary.The only test that counts. What they ask about is what is missing.
