Practical guide · shapes, colours, arrows

Making a diagram that reads

The state-of-the-art note says what the research establishes. This one says what to do. A single idea runs through it: every visual variable encodes one thing and one only. Shape says what kind of object it is, colour which group it belongs to, size its importance, position the flow. A diagram becomes unreadable the day one of them encodes two.

01What each variable is allowed to say

Shape is the most under-used variable. It is free, it is memorable, it survives a photocopy and colour blindness. Many diagrams use nothing but rectangles and then make colour carry all the meaning — the more fragile of the two.

Making shape carry importance rather than nature. A critical component has no special shape: it has a size, a position, or a colour accent. Otherwise the diagram's grammar contradicts itself.

03Colour encodes one dimension only

The most frequent mistake, and the most expensive

On a real diagram the temptation is constant: two boxes coloured by owning team, a third in red because it is critical, a fourth in blue because it is Java. Each decision is reasonable taken alone. Together they make colour unreadable: the reader can no longer tell what a hue means.

One dimension per variable. If you have three pieces of information to encode — layer, criticality, team — you need three variables: colour, stroke, position. Not three uses of colour.

04Grey carries the structure, colour carries the point

A fully coloured diagram has no subject

A diagram almost always exists to say one thing: here is the path of a request, here is the component being added, here is where it breaks. If everything is coloured, nothing is. The rule that works: structure in neutrals, and colour reserved for what you are talking about.

A good proportion: two thirds of the diagram in neutrals. The six layer colours only come out when the diagram is really about layers — on a diagram telling a flow, they are noise.

05The seven colour rules

In the order in which they get broken

  • Never colour alone. Any information carried by a hue must also be carried by a word, a shape or a position. That is WCAG criterion 1.4.1, and it is also what saves the diagram at the photocopier.
  • Six categories at most. Beyond that, the reader goes back to the legend at every box, and colour stops being a shortcut.
  • Red is reserved. It means problem, failure, danger — in every technical culture. Spending it on “Payments team” is shouting for no reason.
  • The fill stays neutral — but neutral does not mean identical. Grey values encode shape and nesting, and they have to be genuinely separated: our first four fills sat between 1.05 and 1.08:1 against white, which is not weak contrast but the absence of contrast. A projector rendered them all as white. The scale now runs from 1.09:1 for a zone to 1.27:1 for a deployment node.
  • The accent is not a layer. One colour, and one only, is reserved for “this is the subject of this diagram”: it never designates a domain, it is absent from the layer palette, and the legend must name it separately — otherwise the reader looks for it among the layers. One accented box per diagram: beyond that it designates nothing. And because a magenta and a teal become the same colour under deuteranopia, the accent also carries a thicker stroke: the rule “never colour alone” applies to it first.
  • Contrast: 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for lines. A tired projector eats about a third of the contrast; what barely passes on screen will disappear in a meeting room.
  • The fill colour is soft, the stroke colour is firm. A saturated flat crushes the text laid on it; a 10–15 % tint with a solid stroke keeps both readable.

06Arrows

Where the real meaning hides

Boxes say what exists; arrows say how the system works. And yet they are where the effort slackens.

  • Name them. “reads the orders from” carries information; “uses” carries none. If the name does not come, it usually means the relationship is not clear in your own head.
  • Point them at whoever receives the call, not at wherever the data comes from. The direction of the arrow answers “who calls whom”, that is, who has to know about the other.
  • Banish the double-headed arrow. It hides exactly who takes the initiative. Two distinct arrows, or one, with the return implied.
  • Solid against dashed: one duality per diagram. Synchronous / asynchronous, or nominal / degraded, but not both — and always in the legend.
  • Orthogonal in a dense diagram, direct when it is airy. And above all: cross as little as possible, that is the factor weighing most on comprehension.
Line weight to say “lots of traffic”. Tempting and almost always wrong: nobody can read three weights, and weight is already used to tell the subject from the rest. Write the throughput on the arrow.

07Spacing says who goes with whom

The variable everyone thinks is decorative

Two boxes close together form a group in the reader's eyes. It is involuntary and immediate, and it happens even if you did not intend it — so irregular spacing lies.

  • Align to a grid — 10 px in draw.io, on by default. A three-pixel offset shows, and reads as an intention.
  • The gap between two groups must be at least twice the gap inside a group. That is the rule that makes a grouping obvious without drawing a single frame.
  • One box width per level. Variable widths read as a hierarchy that does not exist.
  • Let the edges breathe. A generous outer margin makes the diagram look composed rather than endured.

08The decisive test

Thirty seconds, before you send it

The recipe, in order

  1. Write first what the diagram has to make clear.One sentence. If you cannot, the diagram has no subject and no styling will save it.
  2. Lay the boxes, with no colour.Shapes and positions only. The diagram must already be readable at this stage.
  3. Remove the arrow crossings.Before any decoration. It is the heaviest lever.
  4. Name every arrow.If the verb does not come, the relationship is not clear.
  5. Add the zone frames.Network, trust, deployment. Often, colour becomes unnecessary here.
  6. Add colour last, for one dimension only.And keep two thirds of the diagram in neutrals.
  7. Legend, title, acronyms expanded.The diagram must be understood without you beside it.
  8. Desaturate, then have someone from outside read it.What they ask about is what is missing.
